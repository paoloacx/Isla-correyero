Eros y cicuta

José Antonio Llera

On Mi bien. Antología poética (Madrid, Visor, 2018)

Isla Correyero’s work breaks with many of the clichés often attached to the poetry of her generation. I do not think we should approach it under the umbrella of autobiography: autobiography is a way of reading and, moreover, it entails distortion, a kind of fiction. As a reader, I find other ways of looking more rewarding. I prefer to speak of the fierce, visceral Eros that defines this poetry, an eroticism that moves beyond idealism and into a radical exposure of the body, a place of transgression—moral, aesthetic or ideological. I like to think of it through what Roland Barthes called texts of bliss: literature that unsettles boundaries, overflowing continuously in an excess that both threatens and keeps watch.

The first of her books I came across was Diario de una enfermera (1996), a book about illness and compassion for those who suffer. Its pages hold blue urine, white laceration and enslavement to the body. In illness we are all hostages to our own bodies, which strip identity away until it drowns in pure biology. The reader recognises themselves in its prayer and its blood blisters. The nurse arrives with lips made antitetanus, and death with the smell of a decomposing hare.

Crímenes (1993), another key title, moved me just as deeply. I encountered poetry’s serrated teeth, victim and perpetrator set within a framework of taut dramatic monologues. Between the hazards of cruelty, guilt and tenderness appeared. I thought of Thomas de Quincey’s Society of the Experts in Murder, certain poems by Fonollosa and Foucault’s infamous men. I speak of affinities and convergences, not imitation. Divorcio (2015) is one of the few books I know to address male violence in its entirety. Yet it goes further: from it emanates a pleading need for the other.

Mi bien, the anthology published by Visor, feels almost like a new book—not only as a welcome or celebration, but because Isla’s work has received an unusual response. There may be several explanations, but I think one has to do with the dominant canon and its horizon of expectations. This poetry and its expressionist imagination—its plasticity and grotesquerie, its plunder and bonfires of flesh—cannot be accommodated within tepid, conversational sentimentality. Cast to the margins for its unorthodoxy, it now returns, as the repressed so often does. The title Mi bien suggests not only refuge or inviolate intimacy, but a cathartic centre. It does good to writer and reader alike; it has purgative effects, like Attic tragedy. I think of Jack Gilbert’s lines in “In Dispraise of Poetry”, where poetry appears as a white elephant: it demands great care and responsibility, and for that reason is the most dangerous of gifts.

The anthology closes with a luminous poem titled “Luz de agosto bajo el nogal”. After devastated landscapes, anger, betrayal and hemlock, we read a song of resurrection, “after having been covered in ice”. It is another way of naming the bats of melancholy. It would be superficial to say that it finally places us in a locus amoenus, offering a scene of communion with nature on an August afternoon. It is a poem about the end of mourning. It opens a breach in time and records how what was lost has been incorporated—painfully—into the self. Only when that work is done and the loss assimilated can something new emerge. Thus Mi bien makes a journey visible and leaves us waiting for what has yet to be written.
